\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Financial Market Returns}
    \section{Financial Market Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Total Returns}

    Definition of \textbf{total return}:
    $$\text{Return (\%)} = \frac{\text{Income}}{\text{Initial Price}} + \frac{\text{Change in Price}}{\text{Initial Price}} $$

    \vspace{1em}
    Notes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item This is a \textit{backwards looking} formula.   
        \item Calculating total return requires three values: beginning and ending price and income.   Given total return and two of the three values, could calculate the third value by rearranging the equation.
        \item Expressed as a percentage of the initial price.
        \item Don't forget income!  Only include income that was earned during the holding period.
    \end{itemize}  
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Income}
    \framesubtitle{Timing of Income}

    Be careful with the timing of income.   
    \begin{itemize}
      \item In order to receive a dividend, you must purchase the stock
    before the ``ex-dividend'' date.
    \item If you purchase a stock on or after the ``ex-dividend'' date,
    you will not receive the upcoming dividend; the seller retains the
    right to the dividend.
    \item A problem for this class may say "you purchase a stock that just paid a dividend." 
    Do NOT count that dividend in the total return, as you purchased it \textit{after} the dividend was paid.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Real v. Nominal Returns}
    \section{Real v Nominal Returns}
    \framesubtitle{What Is Inflation?}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Inflation is a sustained increase in the general
        price level of goods and services in an economy.

        \item Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money:
        a dollar buys fewer goods and services when prices are higher.

        \item Inflation is typically measured using a price index,
        which tracks the cost of a representative basket of goods and services.

        \item The inflation rate is the percentage change in the
        price index over time:
        \[
        \pi_t = \frac{P_t-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}
        \]

        \item In the U.S., common measures include the
        Consumer Price Index (CPI) and the
        Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Real v. Nominal Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Definitions}

    \textbf{Definitions}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Nominal returns}: the percentage change in the value of an investment measured in current dollars.   Not adjusted for inflation.
        \item \textbf{Real returns}: Returns that are adjusted for inflation.   Real returns are the equivalent to the change in purchasing power.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If prices increase, the real returns will be less than the nominal returns.   Think about an investment account that increases in value.   That's good!   You may be able to buy more stuff.  But, if prices also increase, then you will be able to buy less stuff than you otherwise would, which is less good.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. History}
    \framesubtitle{Using historical returns}

    Often people use historical returns to predict expected returns.   This can be problematic.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Financial returns often have ‘fat tails’
        \item Observations may not be independent
        \item The variance of returns may change over time
        \item Historical returns have no measure of value
    \end{itemize}
    
\end{frame}
