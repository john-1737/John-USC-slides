\begin{foundframe}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Utility and Indifference Curves}
    \section{Utility and Indifference Curves}
    \framesubtitle{Concave Utility}

    Utility functions are generally \textit{concave} with respect to wealth.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    This reflects \textit{diminishing marginal utility} with respect wealth.\\
    \vspace{1em} 
     Intuitively, if a household goes from, say, making \$50,000 to \$150,000 annually that can 
    result in a material change in standard of living.   In contrast, moving from 
    \$100,000,000 to \$100,100,000 probably doesn't matter as much.

\end{foundframe}

\begin{foundframe}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Utility and Indifference Curves}
    \framesubtitle{Expected Values}

   \textbf{Definition:} Expected value is the probability-weighted average of all possible outcomes
    $$E[X] = \sum_{i} p_i \cdot x_i$$
    where $p_i$ is the probability of outcome $x_i$
    \vspace{1em}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Linearity:} $E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]$
        \item \textbf{Constant:} $E[c] = c$ for any constant $c$
    \end{itemize}
    \vspace{1em}

\end{foundframe}

\begin{foundframe}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Utility and Indifference Curves}
    \framesubtitle{Expected Values and Concave Utility}

    It follows from the concavity of the utility function that for a given set of 
    outcomes represented by $x$:
    $$\mathbb{E}[u(x)] < u(\mathbb{E}[x])$$
    In words, the expected utility over a set of uncertain outcomes is lower than 
    the utility of the expected value over those same outcomes.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    In even simpler words: people prefer certain outcomes over uncertain outcomes.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    This is known as 'Jensen's inequality' and holds for any concave function.\\
    \vspace{1em}

\end{foundframe}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Preferences over Return and Risk}
    \framesubtitle{Indifference Curves}

    Typically investors prefer more returns and less risk.   For this lecture, we assume the following equation describes an investor's preferences (or their utility):
    $$U = E[r] - 0.5 \cdot A \cdot \sigma^2$$ 

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Utility is increasing in $E[r]$ and decreasing in $\sigma^2$.   
        \item The risk term in this equation is the variance ($\sigma^2$), whereas typically we refer to an investment's standard deviation ($\sigma$).   The variance is the \textit{square} of the standard deviation.  This is an important detail.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{foundframe}
    \frametitle{3. Two Asset Portfolio: No Correlation}
    \framesubtitle{}

    Let $X$ and $Y$ be two assets with expected returns $E[r_x]$ and $E[r_y]$ and standard deviations $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$.  
    \vspace{1em}
    
    Assume that the two assets are uncorrelated.   (This is an important assumption that we will revisit soon).
    \vspace{1em}

    A portfolio with $w_x$ invested in $X$ and $w_y$ invested in $Y$ will have:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $E[r_p] = w_x E[r_x] + w_y E[r_y]$
        \item $\sigma_p^2 = w_x^2 \sigma_x^2 + w_y^2 \sigma_y^2$
        \item $\sigma_p = \sqrt{ w_x^2 \sigma_x^2 + w_y^2 \sigma_y^2}$
    \end{itemize}

    \blfootnote{We touched on the additive properties of independent (uncorrelated) random variables briefly in an earlier lecture on the normal distribution.}
    
\end{foundframe}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Capital Allocation Line}
    \section{Capital Allocation Line}
    \framesubtitle{Set up}

    Assume, for the moment, that as an investor you have two options
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A risky asset with expected return $E[r_{risky}]$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{risky}$
        \item A risk-free asset (e.g. cash) with expected return $r_f$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{r_f} = 0$
    \end{itemize}
    Your only decision is how much of your portfolio to allocate to the risky asset.   Let the amount invested in the risky portfolio be $w$, leaving $(1-w)$ invested in cash.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    The expected return of your portfolio is $E[r_p] = wr_{risky} + (1+w)r_f$.   The standard deviation of your portfolio is $\sigma_p = w\sigma_{risky}$.   The fact that $\sigma_{r_f} = 0$ simplifies the standard deviation term.
    \vspace{1em}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Capital Allocation Line}
    \section{Capital Allocation Line}
    \framesubtitle{Definition}

    The \textbf{Capital Allocation Line (CAL)}  depicts all possible combiations in risk-return space available from this decision.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item When $w=0$, the portfolio is entirely in cash with $E[r_p] = r_f$ and $\sigma_p = 0$.   Thus the CAL intersects the y-axis at the risk-free rate of return $r_f$.
    \item When $w=1$, the portfolio is entirely invested in the risky asset with $E[r_p] = wr_{risky} + (1-w)r_f$ and $\sigma_p = \sigma_{risky}$. The CAL thus goes through and goes the point $(x=\sigma_{risky},y=(r_{risky}+r_{risk-free}))$
    \end{itemize}
     

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Capital Allocation Line}
    \framesubtitle{The Sharpe Ratio}

    Note that the slope of the Capital Allocation Line is the Sharpe ratio of the risky asset
    $$\frac{E[r_{risky}] - r_f}{\sigma_{r_f}}$$
    \vspace{1em}
    Nice!

\end{frame}

\begin{practiceframe}
    \frametitle{3. Capital Allocation Line}
    \framesubtitle{The Sharpe Ratio}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Can the Sharpe Ratio of any combination of the risky asset and the risk-free asset be different from the ratio of the risky asset taken alone?
        \item The Sharpe Ratio is also referred to as the reward-to-volatility ratio.  Holding the risk-free asset reduces both expected excess return and volatility, but does not change the reward-to-volatility ratio.  Why, then, might an investor choose to hold some of their wealth in the risk-free asset?
    \end{itemize}
    
\end{practiceframe}
