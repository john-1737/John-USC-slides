\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \section{Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \framesubtitle{Dividend Policy}

    Company managers choose how to use earnings.  They can distribute the earnings to 
    the company's owners through dividends.  The share of earnings paid out as dividends is referred to as the \textbf{dividend payout ratio}.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    Alternatively, they can reinvest in the business.  The share of earnings reinvested in the business is the \textbf{earnings retention ratio} or the \textbf{plowback ratio (b)}.   (The plowback ratio is equal to one minus the dividend payout ratio.)\\
    \vspace{1em}
    Dividends in period $i$ are then a function of earnings $E$ and the plowback ratio ($b$):
    $$D_i = E_i (1- b)$$
    \vspace{1em}
    
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \framesubtitle{Dividend Policy}

    The dividend policy then determines the amount of reinvestment in the business, and therefore the amount of growth in the business.  
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Let the return on equity be $ROE$
        \item The amount of equity increases each period by the amount of retained earnings.  $E\times b$
        \item The rate at which equity increases is then $ROE \times b$
        \item If $ROE$ and $b$ remain constant, earnings and dividends grow at the same rate as equity.
        \item Therefore: $$g = ROE \times b$$
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \framesubtitle{Dividend Policy}

    How does company management determine how much to pay in dividends?\\
    \vspace{1em}
    For a given level of earnings, consider:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Higher dividends may increase the value of the company, per the Gordon Growth Model, if the growth rate rate remains constant.
        \item However, the growth rate won't remain constant.   Instead, higher dividends mean less reinvestment in the company, which means less growth.
        \item Optimal dividend payout ratios must balance both considerations
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \framesubtitle{Dividend Policy}

    Using the same notation for $ROE$ and $b$, we can write $D_1 = E_1(1-b)$ where $E_1$ is earnings.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    $$P_0 = \frac{E_1(1-b)}{k - ROE \times b}$$
    Note that $b$ is both in the numerator and in the denominator, so it is not immediately clear how changing $b$ will affect $P_0$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \framesubtitle{Dividend Policy}

    Instead of taking a derivative to see the impact of a change of $b$ on $P_0$, we instead consider three cases:
    \vspace{1em}

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Case 1: $ROE = k$}: The denominator becomes $k(1-b)$ 
        and the price reduces to $P_0 = \frac{E_1}{k}$.  The price is 
        therefore independent of $b$ and equal to a perpetuity.   
        Intuitively, investors are indifferent between the company reinvesting or returning the earnings, because the returns are the same either way.
        \item \textbf{Case 2: $ROE > k$} This is a company has attractive reinvestment opportunities.   Investors will prefer that it pursues those opportunities, so a higher $b$ will lead to a higher stock price.
    \end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

        opportunities.   Investors will prefer that it return earnings through higher dividends, so a lower $b$ will lead to a higher stock price (like the solid line in the preceding chart)
    \end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Modeling Dividend Growth}
    \framesubtitle{No Growth and PVGO}

    If the firm pays out all of its earnings in dividends, then there will be no growth in the firm.   In that case, $D_i = E_i$ for all periods and $g=0$.  The Gordon Growth Model becomes a perpetuity:

    $$V_0 = \frac{E_1}{k}$$

    The \textbf{present value of growth opportunities (PVGO)}
    is the difference between the value of the stock and its value in the no-growth scenario. 

    \begin{align*}
        Price &= \text{No-growth value of firm} + PVGO\\
        P_0 &= \frac{E_1}{k} + PVGO
    \end{align*}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Dividends and Buybacks}
    \section{Dividends and Buybacks}
    \framesubtitle{Dividends}

    \framesubtitle{Dividends}

    \textbf{Dividends} are occasional payments made by a company to its shareholders. 
    Dividends are discretionary (no contractual obligation to pay).\\
    \vspace{1em}

    \textit{Why should we use dividends for valuation?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Theoretically, all profits must be paid out to shareholders at some point
        \item It's a starting point that can be augment easily.
    \end{itemize}

    \textit{Why shouldn't we use dividends for valuation?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Companies can also distribute cash through share buybacks
        \item What about companies that don't pay dividends?
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Dividends and Buybacks}
    \framesubtitle{Buybacks}

    Recently company managers have preferred to return earnings to shareholders 
    via \textbf{stock buybacks} rather than through dividends.  Buybacks benefit 
    all shareholders through higher prices.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    Advantages of stock buybacks include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Investors treat dividends as 'sticky'.  Any changes to dividend policy
        are scrutinized.   In contrast, stock buybacks are more likely to be considered 
        one-offs, which makes them a good way to distribute excess cash.
        \item Management may view its stock price as undervalued.
        \item There may be different tax treatment between dividends and capital gains
        \item Reduces the amount of equity outstanding, which could improve financial ratios (Earnings per share, Return on Equity)
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Dividends and Buybacks}
    \framesubtitle{Controversy and Recent Regulation}

    Despite some advantages, stock buybacks are controversial:\\
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Prioritizes stock price gains over investments (R\&D, capital expenditures)
        \item Can artificially inflate EPS without improving underlying business performance
        \item Benefits executives with stock-based compensation more than other stakeholders
    \end{itemize}
    \vspace{1em}
    Heightened public awareness led to some recent changes in tax and regulatory treatment:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Inflation Reduction Act (2022): 1\% excise tax on share repurchases by public companies
        \item SEC enhanced disclosure requirements (2023): more detailed reporting on buyback timing and rationale
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Free Cash Flow Models}
    \section{Free Cash Flow Models}
    \framesubtitle{Overview}

    Free cash flow models value a company by discounting its
    expected future cash flows to the present.  Unlike the Dividend Discount Model, these models do not rely on actual dividend payments. This addresses the concern that some companies pay little or no dividends, and dividend policy may not reflect the cash
    generated by the business.

    \vspace{1em}

    \textbf{Two approaches:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{FCFF}: Cash available to debt and equity holders.
        Discount using the weighted average cost of capital (WACC).
        \item \textbf{FCFE}: Cash available to equity holders.
        Discount using the cost of equity ($r_E$).
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{1em}

    \textbf{Important:} The discount rate must match the type
    of cash flow being valued.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. The Income Statement}
    \framesubtitle{Income Statement}

    The \textbf{income statement} summarizes a company's revenues,
    expenses, and profits over a period of time.

    \vspace{1em}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Revenue}: money earned from selling goods and services.

        \item \textbf{Expenses}: costs incurred to generate revenue.

        \item \textbf{Gross profit}: revenue minus the direct costs of
        producing the goods or services sold.

        \item \textbf{Operating income}: gross profit minus operating
        expenses such as R\&D, sales, and administration.

        \item \textbf{Net income}: profit remaining after operating expenses,
        interest, taxes, and other non-operating items.

        \item The income statement measures \textbf{flows over a period},
        unlike the balance sheet, which is a snapshot at a point in time.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. The Cash Flow Statement}
    \framesubtitle{Cash Flow Statement}

    The \textbf{cash flow statement} summarizes a company's
    cash inflows and outflows over a period of time.

    \vspace{1em}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Operating activities}: Cash generated by
        the company's core business operations.

        \item Starts with net income and adjusts for noncash
        expenses (such as depreciation) and changes in
        working capital.

        \item \textbf{Investing activities}: Cash used for or
        generated by investments, including purchases of
        property, plant, and equipment (CapEx).

        \item \textbf{Financing activities}: Cash raised from or
        paid to investors, including borrowing, debt repayments,
        dividends, and share repurchases.

        \item Unlike the income statement, the cash flow statement
        tracks \textbf{cash rather than accounting earnings}.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Free Cash Flow Models}
    \framesubtitle{Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)}

    \textbf{Definition:} Cash flow available to all capital providers (debt and equity holders) before any financing costs.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    \textbf{Calculation:}
    \begin{align*}
    FCFF &= \text{EBIT}(1-\text{Tax Rate}) + \text{Depreciation} \\
         &\quad - \text{Capital Expenditures} - \text{Change in Working Capital}
    \end{align*}
    \vspace{1em}
    \textbf{Discount Rate:} $WACC$ is the weighted average cost of capital
    \textbf{Valuation:}
    $$\text{Firm Value} = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{FCFF_t}{(1 + WACC)^t}$$
    \vspace{1em}
    \textbf{Use Case:} Total firm valuation; subtract debt to get equity value

    \blfootnote{EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Free Cash Flow Models}
    \framesubtitle{Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)}

    \textbf{Definition:} Cash flow available to equity holders after all expenses, taxes, debt payments, and reinvestment needs.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    \textbf{Calculation:}
    \begin{align*}
    FCFE = FCFF &- \text{Interest Expense}(1-\text{Tax Rate}) \\
        &+ \text{Increases in net debt}
    \end{align*}
    \vspace{1em}
    \textbf{Discount Rate:} $r_E$ is the required return on equity (cost of equity) 
    \textbf{Valuation:} 
    $$\text{Equity Value} = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{FCFE_t}{(1 + r_E)^t}$$

    \textbf{Use Case:} Direct valuation of equity, particularly useful for leveraged firms

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Free Cash Flow Models}
    \framesubtitle{So, FCFF or FCFE?}

    \textbf{FCFF versus FCFE:}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item FCFF values the entire firm; FCFE values equity directly.

        \item To obtain equity value from FCFF, subtract net debt
        (debt minus excess cash) from the enterprise value.

        \item Both approaches should produce the same equity value
        when cash flows and discount rates are consistent.

        \item These are just two different approaches to the same problem.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Free Cash Flow Models}
    \framesubtitle{Modigliani and Miller}

    The Modigliani-Miller theorem states that in perfect markets,
    a firm's value is independent of its capital structure
    (debt vs. equity mix).
    \vspace{1em}

    \textbf{Intuition:}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Debt is generally cheaper than equity, so increasing
        leverage initially appears to reduce financing costs.

        \item However, greater leverage makes equity riskier,
        increasing the required return on equity.

        \item In the MM model, this increase exactly offsets
        the apparent benefit of cheaper debt.

        \item Therefore, the \textbf{weighted average cost of capital
        (WACC) remains unchanged} as leverage changes.
    \end{itemize}

    \blfootnote{    The formal result relies on restrictive assumptions, including
    no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, and frictionless capital markets.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Free Cash Flow Models}
    \framesubtitle{Implications of Modigliani-Miller}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item In perfect capital markets, firm value depends on
        underlying operating cash flows, not financing decisions (WACC is independent of leverage).

        \item Changes in capital structure affect the risk and
        required returns of debt and equity, but not total firm value.

        \item This provides a useful theoretical foundation for
        understanding the relationship between financing and valuation.

        \textit{However, in practice, WACC may change as leverage changes, because of taxes, bankruptcy costs, and other market imperfections. Thus, capital structure may affect firm value.}
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}
