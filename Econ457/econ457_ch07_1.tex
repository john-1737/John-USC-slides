\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{Diversification}

    In the previous section we considered the problem of constructing an optimal portfolio from a single risky asset and cash.   This set-up simplified things because (a) cash is uncorrelated with the risky asset, and (b) there was only one choice variable (namely, the weight $w$ in the risky asset), which made maximization pretty easy.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    This week we consider the more general problem of constructing an optimal portfolio from many assets.   We first need to think about correlations (today) and then come up with an approach for maximization (next lecture).

\end{frame}


\begin{foundframe}
    \frametitle{Correlations}
    \section{Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Definitions}

    For two variables $X$ and $Y$ the Expected Value, Variance, and Covariance are defined as follows:\\

    \begin{itemize}
        \item   \textbf{Expected Value}: $E[X]$
        \item   \textbf{Variance of X}: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X-E[X])^2]$
        \item   \textbf{Covariance of X and Y}: $\operatorname{Cov}(X) = E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]$
    \end{itemize}

    It simplifies the math a bit to subtract the expected value from all of the variables.   Let $\tilde{X}$ be the de-meaned value of $X$: $\tilde{X} = X - E[X]$.   Using $\tilde{X}$ the definitions become:
        \begin{itemize}
        \item   \textbf{Expected Value}: $E[\tilde{X}] = 0$
        \item   \textbf{Variance of X}: $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[\tilde{X}^2]$
        \item   \textbf{Covariance of X and Y}: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = E[\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}]$
    \end{itemize}

    \blfootnote{In practice, demeaning the variable requires first estimating the mean (average) and then subtracting that from all of the observations.}

\end{foundframe}

\begin{foundframe}
    \frametitle{Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{The Sum of Two Variables}

    We are now in position to write down the Expected Value and Variance of a sum of two variables.   Let $P=X+Y$ then using the rules of expected values and the definitions from the previous slide, we have:\\
    \vspace{1em}

    \textbf{Expected Value}: $E[P] = E[X+Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$
    \begin{align*}
     \textbf{Variance}:   \operatorname{Var}(P) &= E[\tilde{P}^2]\\
                &= E[(\tilde{X} + \tilde{Y})^2] \\
                &= E[(\tilde{X}^2 + 2\tilde{X}\tilde{Y} + \tilde{Y}^2]\\
                &= E[\tilde{X}^2] + E[2\tilde{X}\tilde{Y}] + E[\tilde{Y}^2]\\
                &= \operatorname{Var}(X) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)
    \end{align*}
    

\end{foundframe}

\begin{foundframe}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Two Asset Portfolio}

    Now we are ready to calculate the expected value and variance of two asset portfolio.  Let $X$ and $Y$ are two assets returns given by $r_x$ and $r_y$.   The expected value of the returns is $E[r_x]$ and $E[r_y]$ and variance of the returns is $\sigma_x^2$ and $\sigma_y^2$\\
    \vspace{1em}
    A portfolio constructed by combining $X$ and $Y$, with weights $w_X$ and $w_Y$ has the following expected value and variance \\
    \vspace{1em}
    
    \textbf{Case 1:} The returns of $X$ and $Y$ are \textit{uncorrelated}
    \begin{align*}
        E[r_p] &= E[w_xr_x + w_yr_y] = E[w_xr_x] + E[w_yr_y]\\
        \operatorname{Var}(r_p) &= w_x^2\sigma_x^2 + w_y\sigma_y^2
    \end{align*}
    Note the $w_x^2$ and $w_y^2$ terms, which are a result of the definition of variance on the previous slide.

\end{foundframe}

\begin{foundframe}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Two Asset Portfolio, Continued}
    
    \textbf{Case 2:} The returns of $X$ and $Y$ are \textit{correlated} 
      \begin{align*}
            E[r_p] &= E[w_xr_x + w_yr_y] = E[w_xr_x] + E[w_yr_y]\\
        \operatorname{Var}(r_p) &= w_x^2\sigma_x^2 + w_y\sigma_y^2 +2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)
    \end{align*}
    The \textit{correlation coefficient} is defined as:
    $$\rho_{x,y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(x,y)}{\sigma_x\sigma_y}$$

    We can rewrite the Variance of a two asset portfolio using $\rho_{x,y}$
    $$\operatorname{Var}(r_p) &= w_x^2\sigma_x^2 + w_y\sigma_y^2 +2\sigma_x\sigma_y\rho_{x,y}$$
    
    It is mostly a matter of convenience whether you use the correlation coefficient or the covariance.
    
\end{foundframe}


\begin{foundframe}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Two Asset Portfolio}

    A note of caution before we proceed: everything in the lecture up to this point has referenced the \textit{variance}.   In most applications, we are going to be interested in the \textit{standard deviation}, so we will need to take a square root.
    $$\sigma_p = \sqrt{\sigma_p^2}$$
    
\end{foundframe}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. The Benefit of Diversification}
    \section{The Benefit of Diversification}

    Diversification reduces the standard deviation of a portfolio.   Consider two assets $x$ and $y$ that have returns with the same variance: $\sigma^2_X = \sigma^2_Y = \sigma^2$.   A portfolio composed of either asset by itself has a standard deviation equal to $\sigma$.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    Consider a portfolio that is $\frac{1}{2}$ asset $x$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ asset $y$.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item If the two assets are uncorrelated, the variance of the portfolio will be:
    $$0.5^2\sigma + 0.5^2\sigma^2 = 0.25\sigma^2 + 0.25\sigma^2 = 0.5\sigma^2$$  
    The standard deviation will be $\sqrt{0.5}\sigma = 0.7\sigma$.   That is a $\approx30\%$ reduction in the standard deviation, relative to a portfolio composed of only one of the assets!
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. The Benefit of Diversification}

    Continued
    \begin{itemize}
    \item If the two assets are correlated, the variance of the portfolio will be:
    \begin{align*}
        0.5^2\sigma &+ 0.5^2\sigma^2 + 2\cdot0.5\cdot0.5\cdot \text{Cov}(x,y) \\
        &=0.5^2\sigma + 0.5^2\sigma^2 + 2\cdot0.5\cdot0.5\cdot\sigma^2\rho_{x,y} \\
    &= 0.25\sigma^2 + 0.25\sigma^2 + 0.5 \sigma^2\rho_{x,y}\\
    &=0.5\sigma^2 + 0.5 \sigma^2 \rho_{x,y}
    \end{align*}
    The amount of reduction in the standard deviation depends on $\rho_{x,y}$.  
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Estimating Correlations}
    \section{Estimating Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Variance-Covariance Matrix}

    The \textbf{Variance-Covariance matrix} of $X$ and $Y$ is as follows:
    $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \sigma_x^2 & \text{Cov}(X,Y) \\
    \text{Cov}(X,Y) & \sigma_y^2
    \end{pmatrix}$$

    where:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\sigma_x^2$ is the variance of $X$
        \item $\sigma_y^2$ is the variance of $Y$  
        \item $\text{Cov}(X,Y)$ is the covariance between $X$ and $Y$
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Estimating Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Empirical Variance-Covariance Matrix}

    \footnotesize

    Given observations $(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \ldots, (x_T, y_T)$, the empirical variance-covariance matrix is:

    $$\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \begin{pmatrix}
    \hat{\sigma}_x^2 & \widehat{\text{Cov}}(X,Y) \\
    \widehat{\text{Cov}}(X,Y) & \hat{\sigma}_y^2
    \end{pmatrix}$$

    where:
    \begin{align}
    \hat{\mu}_x^2 &= \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x)\\
    \hat{\sigma}_x^2 &= \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t - \hat{\mu})^2\\
    \widehat{\text{Cov}}(X,Y) &= \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t - \hat{\mu_x})(y_t - \hat{\mu_y})
    \end{align}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{3. Estimating Correlations}
    \framesubtitle{Variance-Covariance Matrix}

    An alternative to using historical data is to use estimated probabilities for the various outcomes. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The historical data implicitly gives equal weight to each observation (each observation gets a weight of $\frac{1}{N}$)
        \item If you instead use estimated probabilities, the weights of each outcome will be equal to the probability of that outcome occurring.   Be careful to ensure the sum of the weights equal one.
    \end{itemize}
    For example, if there are 2 outcomes $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ which occur with probability $p_1$ and $p_2$, then the estimated covariance would be:
    $$\widehat{\text{Cov}}(X,Y) = p_1(x_1 - \hat{\mu_x})(y_1 - \hat{\mu_y}) + p_2(x_2 - \hat{\mu_x})(y_2 - \hat{\mu_y})$$

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
\frametitle{4. Many Assets}
\framesubtitle{Portfolio Variance}

    \footnotesize
    The general formula for the variance of a portfolio composed of
    $n$ risky assets is:
    
    \[
    \sigma_p^2
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
    w_iw_j\operatorname{Cov}(r_i,r_j)
    \]
    
    For an equally weighted portfolio, $w_i=\frac{1}{n}$, so:
    
    \[
    \sigma_p^2
    =
    \frac{1}{n^2}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
    \operatorname{Cov}(r_i,r_j)
    \]
    
    Separating the variance terms from the covariance terms:
    
    \[
    \sigma_p^2
    =
    \underbrace{\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_i^2}
    _{\text{Variance terms}}
    +
    \underbrace{
    \frac{1}{n^2}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n}
    \sum_{\substack{j=1\\j\neq i}}^{n}
    \operatorname{Cov}(r_i,r_j)
    }_{\text{Covariance terms}}
    \]

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[t]
\frametitle{4. Many Assets}
\framesubtitle{Three Securities}

    For three securities, the expected return is:
    
        \[
        E[r_p]
        =
        w_1E[r_1]
        +w_2E[r_2]
        +w_3E[r_3]
        \]
        
    and the variance is:
    
        \[
        \begin{aligned}
        \sigma_p^2
        ={}&
        w_1^2\sigma_1^2
        +w_2^2\sigma_2^2
        +w_3^2\sigma_3^2\\
        &+2w_1w_2\operatorname{Cov}(r_1,r_2)\\
        &+2w_1w_3\operatorname{Cov}(r_1,r_3)\\
        &+2w_2w_3\operatorname{Cov}(r_2,r_3).
        \end{aligned}
        \]

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{5. SPY and TLT}
    \framesubtitle{Risk Premium}

    A note on terminology: The \textit{risk premium} is the expected 
    excess return of a security: $$\mathbb{E}[r_{SPY}] - r_{f}$$\\
    \vspace{1em}
    
    The historic average excess returns is often used as an estimate of the risk premium.\\
    \vspace{1em}
    Note that the risk premium is the numerator of the Sharpe Ratio.

\end{frame}
