\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Inflation \& Returns}
    \section{Real v Nominal Returns}
    \framesubtitle{What Is Inflation?}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Inflation is a sustained increase in the general
        price level of goods and services in an economy.

        \item Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money:
        a dollar buys fewer goods and services when prices are higher.

        \item Inflation is typically measured using a price index,
        which tracks the cost of a representative basket of goods and services.

        \item The inflation rate is the percentage change in the
        price index over time:
        \[
        \pi_t = \frac{P_t-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}
        \]

        \item In the U.S., common measures include the
        Consumer Price Index (CPI) and the
        Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}



\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Inflation \& Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Definitions}

    \textbf{Definitions}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Nominal returns}: the percentage change in the value of an investment measured in current dollars.   Not adjusted for inflation.
        \item \textbf{Real returns}: Returns that are adjusted for inflation.   Real returns are the equivalent to the change in purchasing power.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If prices increase, the real returns will be less than the nominal returns.   Think about an investment account that increases in value.   That's good!   You may be able to buy more stuff.  But, if prices also increase, then you will be able to buy less stuff than you otherwise would, which is less good.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Inflation \& Real Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Computing Real Returns}

    \vspace{-0.5em}
    Two approaches to calculating real returns for a monthly series:
    \vspace{0.5em}

    \textbf{1. Adjust all dollar values to be ``real''}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For a dollar value recorded in month $y$, convert it to a
        ``real'' value by dividing by
        $\frac{\text{CPI}_y}{\text{CPI}_{initial}}$, where
        $\text{CPI}_y$ is the CPI index on the date the value was recorded
        and $\text{CPI}_{initial}$ is the CPI index in the initial period.

        \item Do this for both prices and dividends.

        \item Calculate total returns now using these ``real''  series.
    \end{itemize}

    \textbf{2. Calculate the real total return each month}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Calculate the nominal total return in the normal way.

        \item Convert the gross return to a real gross return:
        \[
        1+r_t^{real}
        =
        \frac{1+r_t^{nominal}}
        {\text{CPI}_t/\text{CPI}_{t-1}}
        \]

        \item The result is the ``real'' total return for that month.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Inflation \& Real Returns}
    \subsection{Fisher Equation}
    \framesubtitle{Fisher Equation}
    A common approximation* is:
    $$r_\text{real} \approx r_{\text{nominal}} - \pi$$
    where $r_\text{real}$ is the annualized real rate of return, 
    $r_{\text{nominal}}$ is the annualized nominal rate of return, 
    and $\pi$ is the inflation rate over the relevant period.\\
    \vspace{1em}

    Note that this approximation does not work well for large inflations.   In the case of a very high inflation, it is necessary to calculate the real yield explicitly by adjusting the price series.
    
    \vfill
    \footnotesize
    \rule{2in}{0.4pt}\\
    *As long as $\pi$ is small, we can ignore the cross-term because it is close to zero.
    $$(1 + r_{\text{nominal}}) = (1+r_\text{real})(1 + \pi) = 1 + r_\text{real} + \pi + r_\text{real}*\pi \approx 1 + r_\text{real} + \pi$$
    

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Inflation \& Real Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Fisher Equation}

    When used for \textit{expected returns}, this approximation is commonly known as the \textit{Fisher Equation}.\\
    $$\mathbb{E}[r_\text{real}] \approx \mathbb{E}[r_{\text{nominal}}] - \pi^e$$
    Where $\pi^e$ is expected inflation.\\
    \vspace{1em}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Inflation \& Real Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Fisher Equation}

    The Fisher Equation is most commonly used in the context of bonds, where the expected return is equal to the interest rate (much more on this later).  With a little rearranging, we have:
    $$i_{\text{nominal}} \approx i_{\text{real}} + \pi^e$$
    Where $i_{\text{nom}}$ is the nominal interest rate and $i_{\text{real}}$ is the real interest rate. \\
    
    Note that \textit{both} nominal bond yields may change in response to a change in inflation.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Risk-free rates \& Excess Returns}
    \section{Risk-free Returns and Excess Returns}
    Another way to evaluate returns is to compare them against an alternative investment.  A natural comparison is the "risk-free rate of return."  
    
    \vspace{1em}
    The \textit{Excess Return} of an investment is simply:
    $$\text{Excess Return} = \text{Actual Rate of Return} - \text{Risk-Free Rate of Return}$$
    

    One convention is to use the return on Treasury Bills (T-Bills) as the risk-free rate.  This is appropriate because T-bills have no default risk and returns have very low volatility.\\
    \vspace{1em}

    
    \blfootnote{Note that Treasury \textit{bonds} have price risk, 
    and are therefore not good measures of 'risk-free rates'.  More on this later in the class.}
    

\end{frame}

    \framesubtitle{A few words of caution}

    In the discussion about inflation we compared a price series with the CPI.  
    In contrast, here we compare returns of the risky asset with returns on the 
    risk-free asset.\\
    \vspace{1em}

    T-Bill are typically quoted with annualized rates, which need to be adjusted for the 
    appropriate period:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The 3m return: $(1+\text{T-Bill Rate})^\frac{1}{4}-1$, 
      \item The 1m return: $(1+ \text{T-Bill Rate})^\frac{1}{12}-1$\\
    \end{itemize}


\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{2. Risk-free rates \& Excess Returns}
    \framesubtitle{Computing Excess Returns}

    In order to calculate the excess monthly return for an asset:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Calculate the monthly return for the risk-free asset (T-Bills)
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Be careful to adjust any annualized rates, as discussed on the previous slide, to get the monthly return.
      \end{itemize}
      \item Create the monthly total return for the risky asset 
        \begin{itemize}
        \item Remember to include income
      \end{itemize}
      \item For each period, subtract the risk-free return from the return for the risky asset to get the "excess return" of the risky asset.\\


    \end{enumerate}
    
\end{frame}

  \framesubtitle{Sharpe Ratio}

  The \textbf{Sharpe Ratio} is defined as:
  $$\frac{E[r] - r_f}{\sigma}$$
  Where $E[r] - r_f$ is the \textit{excess} return of the asset and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation.\\
  \vspace{1em}
  We will see the Sharpe Ratio used in many different contexts in the coming weeks.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
  \frametitle{3. Index Returns \& Excess Returns}
  \framesubtitle{The Information Ratio}

  The \textbf{Information Ratio} is defined as
  $$\frac{E[r - r_{index}]}{\sigma_{active}}$$
  Where $E[r - r_{index}]$ is the \textit{excess} return of the portfolio, measured against an appropriate index, and $\sigma_{active}$ is the standard deviation of the excess returns.
  The standard deviation of active returns is often referred to as the \textit{tracking error} of the portfolio.\\
  \vspace{1em}
  This is very similar to the Sharpe Ratio.   The key difference is that here excess returns are calculated against an index, rather than against the risk-free rate.

\end{frame}
