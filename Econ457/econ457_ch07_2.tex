\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Step 1: Maximize the Sharpe Ratio}
    \section{Step 1: Maximize the Sharpe Ratio}
    \framesubtitle{Intuition}

    What is the optimal combination of two assets?
    \begin{itemize}
        \item It is \textit{not} necessarily the combination that minimizes the variance
        \item Instead, we define the optimal combination as the one that \textit{maximizes the Sharpe Ratio}, which is defined as the expected excess return per unit of volatility:
        $$\frac{\mathbb{E}[r_p] - r_f}{\sigma_p}$$
        \item Intuitively, the Sharpe Ratio is a measure of the efficiency of the portfolio.   The optimal portfolio is therefore the most efficient portfolio.
    \end{itemize}    

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Step 1: Maximize the Sharpe Ratio}
    \framesubtitle{Minimum Variance Portfolio}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item The \textbf{minimum variance portfolio} is constructed by finding weights $w$ that minimize the standard deviation of the portfolio.   
        \item Graphically, it is the leftmost point on the graph in the previous slide. This requires some additional calculus, which we won't do in this class. 
        \item For a portfolio with assets $X$ and $Y$, the weight on $X$ in the minimum variance portfolio is given by
        \footnotesize
        $$w_x = \frac{\sigma_y^2 - Cov(X,Y)}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 -2Cov(X,Y)}$$
        \normalsize
        \item For more assets, we have to use Excel or Python.
        \item The minimum variance portfolio is NOT the optimal portfolio.  The optimal portfolio is the one that maximize the Sharpe Ratio.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Step 1: Maximize the Sharpe Ratio}
    \framesubtitle{Maximization Problem}

    Maximize the Sharpe Ratio of the portfolio:
    $$
    \max_{w_i} S_p = \frac{\mathbb{E}(r_p) - r_f}{\sigma_p}
    $$
    s.t.
    $$
    w_{SPY} + w_{TLT} = 1
    $$

    As we did in the capital allocation problem, the approach is to substitute the expressions $\mathbb{E}(r_p)$, $\sigma_p$ and $w_{TLT}$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{1. Step 1: Maximize the Sharpe Ratio}
    \framesubtitle{Maximization Problem}

    After substitution, the Sharpe Ratio is:
    \footnotesize
    $$
    \frac{w_{SPY}*\mathbb{E}(R_{SPY})+(1-w_{SPY})\mathbb{E}(R_{TLT}) - r_f}{w_{SPY}^{2}*\sigma^{2}_{SPY} + (1-w_{SPY})^{2}*\sigma^{2}_{TLT} + 2*(w_{SPY})*(1-w_{SPY})*Cov(r_{SPY},r_{TLT})}
    $$
    \vspace{1em}
    \normalsize

    Then proceed in the same way:
     \begin{itemize}
         \item Take the derivative with respect to $w$
         \item Set the derivative equal to zero
         \item Finally solve for $w$.
    \end{itemize}
    You will not be asked to do this maximization on a midterm or final.   However, you should understand what is being maximized and why.   See the following practice problem.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
    \frametitle{Summary: Optimal SPY and TLT Portfolio}
    \framesubtitle{For a given investor}

    In order to find the optimal allocation between three assets (SPY, TLT, and cash) for a given investor, follow these steps:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Estimate return and risk characteristics of the risky assets and the risk-free asset
        \item Find optimal allocation between SPY and TLT by maximizing the Sharpe Ratio.  
        \item Find optimal allocation to cash, using indifference curves
    \end{enumerate}
    \vspace{1em}

    Note that all investors will use same weights for SPY and TLT within their allocation to risky assets.  These weights maximize the Sharpe Ratio and produce the most efficient risky portfolio.   Investors will then have different allocations to cash, depending on their personal indifference curves.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
\frametitle{4. Many-Asset Optimization}
\framesubtitle{Procedure}

Steps for constructing an optimal portfolio with many risky assets:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Estimate the vector of expected returns and the
        variance--covariance matrix.
        
        \item Construct the feasible set and identify the mean--variance
        efficient frontier. The Global Minimum-Variance Portfolio is the
        leftmost point on this frontier.
        
        \item Given the risk-free rate, identify the risky portfolio that
        maximizes the Sharpe ratio. This is the tangency portfolio.
        
        \item Draw the Capital Allocation Line through the risk-free asset
        and the tangency portfolio.
        
        \item Choose the point on the Capital Allocation Line that is tangent
        to the investor's highest attainable indifference curve. This
        determines the allocation between the tangency portfolio and cash.
    \end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[t]
\frametitle{4. Many-Asset Optimization}
\framesubtitle{Procedure}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item The framework described on the previous slide is known as
    \textit{Markowitz mean--variance analysis}
    \item This is the foundation of
    "Modern Portfolio Theory". 
    \item Harry Markowitz received the 1990 Nobel Prize in Economics for developing the theory of portfolio choice.
    \end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{excelframe}[t]
    \frametitle{5. Excel}
    \framesubtitle{Correlations (last week's homework)}

    Takeaways

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Correlations are based on [monthly] returns (not a total return index).  The levels may look similar -- they all go up -- but it's the changes that matter.
        \item Correlations take out the mean.  Series A-C have a mean increase of 2\%, but that gets removed 
        in the correlation calculation.  Series D has a mean increase of 4\%, but that also gets removed.
        \item Correlations are disproportionately impacted by the observations with the 
        largest moves.   Series A and C are identical except for observation 50.   That single observation, which is the one with 
        the largest change, drives the negative correlation.
    \end{itemize}

\end{excelframe}
